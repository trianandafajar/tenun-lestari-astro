Mornings in the weaving village start earlier than we expected. Before six, the clack of looms is already drifting from several verandas. Women weave while waiting for water to boil, and yesterday's dyed yarn hangs drying under the mango tree.

## A small meeting on the veranda

This visit had one main item on the agenda: agreeing prices for the next weaving season. We sat with the group leader and the weavers to talk through how long each cloth takes, how much yarn it needs, and what a fair payment looks like. Half of each order's value is paid up front, so artisans don't have to borrow to buy yarn.

## A generation coming back

The most heartening part was meeting three young women who had just joined the group. Two of them had worked in the city and came back because weaving can now be a steady income. They are learning to tie motifs from the senior weavers and are already proposing new colour combinations.

## What we brought home

Besides a few cloths for the rainy-season collection, we came home with a to-do list: noni-dye training, repairs for two ageing looms, and plans for a workshop with the village school children.

Weaving is slow work. But from those verandas we learned that slow doesn't mean left behind.
