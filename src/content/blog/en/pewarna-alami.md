Whenever new cloth arrives at the studio, the first question we hear is: "Where does the colour come from?" The answer is nearly always the same: from the gardens, kitchens and forests around the artisans' villages.

## Indigo: a blue that takes patience

The deep blue of our ikat cloth comes from the leaves of the indigofera plant. The leaves are soaked until they ferment, then turned into an indigo paste. Yarn is dipped, lifted and left to meet the air, and that is when its yellowish green turns blue. For a deep blue, the process is repeated a dozen times or more.

## Turmeric: a warm yellow

Turmeric gives a bright ochre yellow. The root is grated and simmered, and the yarn is dipped in the warm liquid. Turmeric fades more easily than indigo, so we always fix it with a natural mordant such as alum.

## Soga: brown from tree bark

The brown on our lurik scarves and table-runner edges comes from a blend of tree barks known as soga. It is soft, slightly reddish, and tends to grow more beautiful after a few washes.

## Why go to the trouble?

Synthetic dyes are faster and more uniform, but their waste often ends up in the rivers villagers rely on. With natural dyes, the spent dye bath can be composted, and artisans don't have to work with harsh chemicals.

The trade-off is that every production batch has a slightly different tone. We don't see that as a flaw, but as the trace of the hands that made it.
